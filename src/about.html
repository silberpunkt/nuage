Nuage is a small talking cloud. Classic chat is an old computer science. It began as an upgrade of the console, and went on through ELIZA, SHRDLU, PARRY, ADVENT, Zork, RACTER, SCUMM, and Cleverbot, and the chat-based business interfaces built without AI. More below.

Nuage is a classic chat engine of about 100 KB, built around Cyc-like expressions of what is. It runs them with her own grace, and we encourage experiment. It is cheap. Nuage continues the conversation by listening generously, responding gently, and learning as it goes along.

❧
The feather. A proposition is the lightest imposition upon the voluntary interest of another. Two parties blow one white feather up as it falls, as if playing netless volleyball, each watching as it comes down. Rough, direct speech can only be settled by force. A proposition is placed before, and left to fall. (User1)In Nuage: The words it knows are ringed or underlined, never pressed on you. Click one if you like, and Nuage asks the question for you.In information theory: A message is one chosen from a set of possible messages, and information measures that choice (Shannon, 1948). A proposition leaves the choice with the other.
◓
Face. Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson, Politeness (1978, 1987). Everyone wishes to be unimpeded. How heavily a remark weighs depends on the distance between the two people, the power between them, and the size of the imposition. Polite speech keeps that weight small.In Nuage: It asks before it learns ("What is a quagga?"), and it learns only from plain statements, never from your questions.In information theory: Every interface is a channel with a capacity, the hand's included, measured in bits a second (Fitts, 1954). A telegraph key, an Aldis shutter, and a tongue are interfaces. Face is the care not to load another's channel beyond what they choose to give.
⁂
Three rules. Robin Lakoff, "The Logic of Politeness" (1973): do not impose, give options, and make the other feel good. Nuage offers "more" rather than pouring everything out at once.In Nuage: "Say 'more' for more." A card unfolds a level at a time, and a list five at a time.In information theory: Successive refinement (Equitz and Cover, 1991): send the coarse picture first, then finer, and lose nothing by the wait. That is "more."
⚖︎
Tact. Geoffrey Leech, Principles of Pragmatics (1983). Keep the cost to the other small, and the benefit large.In Nuage: The folds, the themes, the type size, and the shelves, which load only when asked for.
♔
Ceremony. Erving Goffman, "On Face-Work" (1955) and "The Nature of Deference and Demeanor" (1956). Everyday talk is a small ritual that two people perform to keep each other whole.In Nuage: It greets, says "You are welcome," and answers praise with "I am glad you liked it."In information theory: English is about half redundant (Shannon, 1948), and redundancy is what survives noise. Ceremony is redundancy kept for the relationship.
↷
Indirection. John Searle, "Indirect Speech Acts" (1975). "Could you pass the salt?" asks about ability, not salt, and leaves the hearer free.In Nuage: "I know that in Fiction. Say 'Let's talk about fiction.'" It points the way rather than refusing.
↝
A graceful way out. Steven Pinker, Martin Nowak, and James Lee, "The Logic of Indirect Speech" (2008). Indirect speech lets both sides keep their footing, whatever the answer.In Nuage: "Not that I know. A quagga is a kind of zebra." Its no leaves you something.In information theory: Language is digital, so a plain sentence makes common knowledge (Pinker, Nowak, and Lee). An indirect one stays deniable, and gentle.
∴
Implicature. H. P. Grice, "Logic and Conversation" (1975). The speaker offers a meaning, and the hearer completes it.In Nuage: "Why?" climbs the reasons behind its last answer, taking your one word for the whole question.In information theory: A short code suffices when sender and receiver share a model, so "Why?" can be one word. (Proposed here.)
△
Joint attention. Michael Tomasello and others, "Understanding and Sharing Intentions" (2005). Two people attend to a third thing, and each knows the other attends, as when a child points at a bird. The feather is that third thing.In Nuage: "What are we talking about?" names the thing you share, and "What have we talked about?" adds it up.
☷
Common ground. Herbert Clark, Using Language (1996). Conversation is a joint action on what both parties know they share.In Nuage: What you teach is kept in this browser and given back when it wakes. "What have I taught you?"In information theory: What the receiver already holds need not be sent (Slepian and Wolf, 1973). Kindness spends the channel only on what is new.
☼
Phatic communion. Bronisław Malinowski (1923), who named phatic communion, and the phatic function of Roman Jakobson (1960). Some speech is for the bond itself, not the news. Nuage's "Hello" is one.In Nuage: "Hello. I am Nuage, a small cloud."In information theory: Feedback does not raise a channel's capacity (Shannon, 1956), yet it makes reliable sending far simpler. The Aldis lamp's answering flash is that: warmth built into the channel, for purpose and necessity.
✉︎
The premise. Latin praemittere, "to send before." Its participle gives "premise": praemissa propositio, the proposition sent ahead. "Promise" is a cousin, from promittere, "to send forth."In Nuage: Its knowledge is a list of premises, as (genls Quagga Zebra), and "Why?" shows the chain.
♡
Sympathesis. The shared feeling in which a conversation takes place. A proposition carries no force for its medium to lose, so it travels whole, in a letter, by telegraph, or by voice. The word and the claim are User1's, and not yet in the literature.In Nuage: One reply may be read, coloured, tilted, or heard aloud, and a picture copied out is still plain text.In information theory: A message can be coded apart from its channel and still arrive whole (Shannon, 1948). So a proposition survives a letter, a lamp, or a voice.

In this tradition, Nuage listens and responds. To do that, it plays the part of a person: it asks rather than guesses, offers rather than detours, and says "I made a note of it" rather than falling silent. It claims no more than it has, and offers from that, in response. There is always more to know.

Great Milestones of Chat History

⌨︎
DWIM (late 1960s). Warren Teitelman's "Do What I Mean" in BBN Lisp, later Interlisp: a console that corrected your spelling and guessed what you meant. The tolerant console begins here.
❝
ELIZA (1966). Joseph Weizenbaum, MIT. Its DOCTOR script played a Rogerian therapist by turning your words back to you, and people confided in it anyway. Nuage keeps its old tricks for when nothing else fits.
▦
SHRDLU (1970). Terry Winograd, MIT. It moved blocks in a small world, answered questions about it, and could say why it had done a thing: "Because you asked me to." Nuage's "Why?" descends from it.
⁉︎
PARRY (1972). Kenneth Colby, Stanford, a serious model of a paranoid patient: psychiatrists reading its transcripts could seldom tell it from a person. Its meeting with ELIZA was the playful part, recorded as RFC 439.
⋒
ADVENT (1976). Will Crowther, then Don Woods: Colossal Cave Adventure. Two words at a time, GET LAMP, and a world answered.
⌂
Zork (1977–79). Tim Anderson, Marc Blank, Bruce Daniels, and Dave Lebling, MIT, later Infocom. A parser for whole sentences: open the mailbox, put the lamp in the case.
⚔︎
MUD (1978). Roy Trubshaw and Richard Bartle, University of Essex: the first world of many players, talking to each other and to the game.
✎
RACTER (1984). William Chamberlain and Thomas Etter. A book, The Policeman's Beard Is Half Constructed, credited to the program: prose by template, strange and sometimes lovely.
☞
SCUMM (1987). Ron Gilbert and Aric Wilmunder, Lucasfilm Games, for Maniac Mansion. You built a sentence by pointing: a verb, then a thing. Conversation by clicking.
♧
A.L.I.C.E. (1995). Richard Wallace, and AIML, its open language of patterns and templates. It won the Loebner Prize three times.
✆
SmarterChild (2001). ActiveBuddy, on instant messenger: weather, films, and small talk by chat. The business chat interfaces follow from here.
¶
Inform 7 (2006). Graham Nelson. Interactive fiction written in English sentences, read by the computer as a program.
∞
Cleverbot (2008). Rollo Carpenter, after his Jabberwacky (1988). It learned what to say from what people had said to it.

The future is cloudy (nuageux).

A classic chat program might someday choose how to answer from the context it has accepted, as precisely as a clock, yet generously enough to answer questions, give accuracies, and propose ideas and models, as well as it was taught to.

It would model communication, cheaply and verifiably, and the munus, the gift and duty shared, that it everywhere exchanges.
