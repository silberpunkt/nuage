<vext> The flag of Norway
  A red field
    charged with
    a dark blue Scandinavian cross
      fimbriated in white.
      The vertical part is shifted to the hoist side,
        in the style of the Dannebrog,
      with an official proportion of
        8:11 for the civil flag,
        16:27 for the state and war flags.
  Designer: Fredrik Meltzer.
    Adoption: approved 13 July 1821
      for use in coastal waters,
      and became the full national flag
        on 10 December 1898.
  Colour elements:
    The cross division follows a ratio
      of 6:1:2:1:12 horizontally
      and 6:1:2:1:6 vertically.
  Colour standards: legally defined as
    deep red and dark blue,
      commonly standardized as
        PMS 200 (red) and
        PMS 281 (blue).
<vext> The flag of Denmark
  A red field
    charged with a white Scandinavian cross,
      its upright shifted to the hoist side.
  Called the Dannebrog, the Danish cloth.
    By legend it fell from the sky
      at the Battle of Lyndanisse, in 1219.
    It is held to be the oldest national flag still in use.
  Proportions: 28:37.
    The cross divides it 12:4:21 across
      and 12:4:12 down.
<vext> The flag of Nepal
  A double pennon, a concave pentagon:
    two right-angled triangular pennons of unequal size,
      the smaller set atop the larger,
      sharing a common upright hoist edge.
  The field is crimson (#DC143C).
    The whole outline is bordered in deep blue (#003893),
      of uniform width.
    The upper pennon bears a white crescent moon,
      with eight of its sixteen rays showing.
    The lower pennon bears a white sun,
      with twelve triangular rays.
  Proportion:
    The crimson field alone is 4 to 3, height to width.
    With the blue border added,
      the height is 1.21901 times the width.
      That number is the least positive root of a quartic polynomial.
        It is irrational, and no simple fraction expresses it.
  Construction:
    The flag is not defined by a fixed ratio.
      It is defined by a compass-and-straightedge construction,
        laid out in Schedule 1 of the Constitution of Nepal (2015).
      It begins with a horizontal baseline,
        and derives every vertex, arc, and the border's width from it.
  Symbolism, by the constitution's text:
    Crimson stands for bravery,
      and for the rhododendron (Rhododendron arboreum),
        the national flower.
    Blue stands for peace.
    The moon stands for the calm, temperate nature
      of the Nepali people.
    The sun stands for their fierce, aggressive temperament.
  Adopted on 16 December 1962.
    Its modern form was standardised by the civil engineer Shankar Nath Rimal,
      at the request of King Mahendra.
<vext> The banner of Jeanne d'Arc
  A vexillum, with a swallowtail.
  A rectangular banner of white boucassin,
    a stiffened linen canvas,
  3 feet high and 12 feet long,
    a proportion of 1 to 4,
  ending in a swallowtail of two points.
  The whole outline is fringed in silk,
    componée, in alternating gold and white threads,
    about 1 inch (2.5 cm) wide.
  The obverse, the "King of Heaven" side:
    The field is sown with gold fleurs-de-lys,
      set parallel to the hoist.
    In the broadest part, nearest the pole,
      is the Apocalyptic Christ, Christ in Majesty.
        He is seated upon a rainbow, within an iridescent golden mandorla,
        His wounds in the hands, feet, and side shown,
        and He wears a light red tunic and a bright red cloak.
    His left hand holds the world, a blue sphere.
      His right hand is raised in blessing.
    Two kneeling angels flank Him,
      in white tunics, with red-and-white wings.
      At His right is Saint Michael, the angel of justice,
        bearing a sword.
      At His left is Saint Gabriel, the angel of mercy,
        presenting a natural lily.
    The words "Jhesus Maria"
      are written in gold letters toward the tail.
  The reverse:
    The Virgin and Child,
      with a shield bearing the arms of France,
        azure, three fleurs-de-lys or,
        held by two angels,
      and a dove bearing in its beak a small streamer,
        inscribed "De par le Roy du Ciel,"
          "By command of the King of Heaven."
  Making and provenance:
    It was commissioned in 1429 from Hamish Power (Hauves Poulnoir),
      a Scottish banner painter in Tours,
      at a cost of 25 livres tournois,
        by the accounts of the Treasurer of War, Hémon Raguier.
  It was blessed at the Church of Saint-Sauveur, in Tours,
      before the relief of Orléans.
    The cloth was of a single thickness,
      so the gold leaf of the lettering and the fleurs-de-lys
        was laid on both faces,
      before the painted figures were laid on the obverse alone.
  Symbolism:
    The white field and the fleurs-de-lys
      assert French sovereignty.
    The Apocalyptic Christ signals divine judgment
      upon the English occupiers.
    The motto, Jhesus Maria, is borrowed from the mendicant orders,
      the Dominicans, Franciscans, Carmelites, and Augustinians,
      who formed the clergy of her army.
    The white banner
      was a deliberate answer to the English red cross,
      signifying that England was subject to France,
        not the reverse.
  Fate:
    The banner was burned during the French Revolution.
    No original survives. Every surviving image is a later reconstruction.
    "I loved my banner forty times better than my sword,"
      said Jeanne at her trial, in 1431.
